01Act 01 · The problem

Ethereum, explained

How do two strangers on opposite sides of the world trust each other with money?

No crypto knowledge needed. We start with a $500 logo.

Lan is a graphic designer in Da Nang. Anna runs a small bakery in Berlin and needs a new logo. They have never met. Scroll down — the picture on the right tells the story.

The oldest problem in trade: who goes first?

If Anna pays first, Lan might disappear with the money. If Lan works first, Anna might take the logo and never pay. Each one is asking the other to take the risk.

So they use a middleman — and pay for it.

A freelance platform holds Anna's money and releases it when the job is done. That works, but it costs: Upwork takes 0–15% from the freelancer per contract, Fiverr keeps 20% of each order.

The platform also makes the rules — it can freeze accounts, change fees, or decide a dispute.

Upwork and Fiverr help centres, as summarised by Lilach Bullock, Oct 2026.

Four ways to settle — watch them one by one.

1. Anna pays first — Anna carries the risk. 2. Lan works first — Lan carries the risk. 3. A platform — safe for both, but $100 of the $500 is gone at Fiverr's 20%.

4. A smart contract — rules written in code, a few cents in fees. The rest of this story explains how that works.

In short

Between strangers, someone has to go first — or a middleman has to hold the money. Ethereum asks: what if the 'middleman' were a small program that nobody owns?

02Act 02 · Origin

A computer nobody owns

QBitcoin already existed. Why build something new?

2013: a 19-year-old has an idea.

Vitalik Buterin was writing about Bitcoin. Bitcoin is like a calculator: brilliant at one thing — sending coins. He asked: what if a blockchain were a full computer, able to run any program?

A smart contract is a vending machine for agreements.

Put in a coin, press B4, get a snack. No shopkeeper decides — the machine's rules do. A smart contract is the same idea in code: 'if this happens, then send that money.'

Once it is on Ethereum, nobody — not even its author — can quietly change the rules.

2014–2015: funded by the public, then switched on.

In a 2014 online sale, people sent about 31,000 BTC (roughly $18 million at the time) and received over 60 million ETH. On 30 July 2015 the network went live.

›Go deeper

A non-profit, the Ethereum Foundation, funds research. But it does not run the network: thousands of independent computers do, and they choose which software to run.

ETH is the fuel.

Every action on this shared computer costs a small fee, paid in ETH. That's why ETH exists — not as 'Ethereum shares', but as the fuel that pays the computers doing the work.

In short

Ethereum is a shared computer run by thousands of people at once. Programs on it — smart contracts — follow their rules automatically, and ETH is the fuel that pays for running them.

03Act 03 · The journey

Eleven years in fast-forward

QWhat has actually happened to Ethereum since 2015?

2015–2017: the first big test.

Within a year, a famous contract called The DAO was drained by a bug. The community voted to undo it — and split into Ethereum and Ethereum Classic.

2017–2020: boom, bust, and 'DeFi summer'.

Start-ups raised billions selling tokens on Ethereum, then most failed. By 2020, real apps for lending and trading without banks were taking off.

2021–2023: burning fees and swapping the engine.

Fees started being burned in 2021. In 2022 'The Merge' replaced mining with staking — while the network kept running.

2024–2026: cheaper, more mainstream, more volatile.

ETFs, cheap Layer-2 networks and a record close near $4,830 in August 2025. By November 2026 ETH was back under $2,000.

Prices: Coin Metrics daily closes to 23 May 2026, then dated news reports (dashed line).

In short

Ethereum survived a hack that split its community, two boom-and-bust cycles and a complete engine swap. The technology kept improving — but the price has never moved in a straight line.

04Act 04 · How it works

The contract in action

QWhat would a smart contract actually do for Lan and Anna?

Anna locks $500 in a contract — not with Lan, not with a platform.

She uses a stablecoin: a digital dollar that stays worth $1, so neither side worries about ETH's price swinging. The money now sits inside a small program on Ethereum that anyone can inspect.

Three rules, written in advance:

1. If Anna presses 'approve', the $500 goes to Lan.

2. If Lan delivers and Anna stays silent for 7 days, the $500 goes to Lan anyway.

3. If nothing is delivered by the deadline, the $500 goes back to Anna.

›Go deeper

Real escrow contracts often add a third person as referee for disputes. That person can be anyone both sides agree on — but then you are trusting a person again.

Three endings, played one after another.

Anna approves after two days → rule 1 pays Lan. Anna stays silent → after 7 days rule 2 pays Lan anyway. Lan never delivers → at the deadline rule 3 refunds Anna.

Nobody presses a button at a company. The fee is a few cents, not a percentage.

Honest limits.

Code cannot tell if a logo is beautiful — only whether a file was delivered on time. And a bug in the code is a bug forever: that is exactly what went wrong with The DAO.

In short

The contract holds the money and follows its rules exactly — no company in between, no fee percentage. But it can only judge what can be checked by code.

A single shared notebook copied onto thousands of computers worldwide; every 12 seconds a new page is added and all copies update together.

05Act 04 · How it works

One notebook, thousands of copies

QWhere does this contract live, and who runs it?

Imagine a notebook that thousands of people keep identical copies of.

Every balance and every contract is written in it. If one copy is changed in secret, it no longer matches the others — and is ignored.

Every 12 seconds, a new page.

New transactions are collected into a 'block' — a page of the notebook — and added to the end. Pages are linked, so old pages can't be edited without breaking the chain.

Lan and Anna's contract is one entry in that notebook.

When Anna presses 'approve', every computer runs the same code and gets the same result. That is why nobody needs to trust a single company.

But running code on thousands of computers is expensive.

What stops someone from uploading a program that loops forever and jams everyone? The answer is the fee — called gas.

In short

Ethereum is one shared record kept by thousands of computers. Every 12 seconds a new page (block) is added. Contracts are just entries in that record — so everyone can check them.

Blocks drawn as buses leaving every 12 seconds. When buses are full the ticket price rises, when they are empty it falls; the base part of every ticket is burned in a small flame.

06Act 04 · How it works

Gas: the ticket price

QWhy is Ethereum sometimes cheap and sometimes very expensive?

Think of each block as a bus that leaves every 12 seconds.

Seats are limited. 'Gas' measures how much work your transaction needs — a simple ETH transfer uses 21,000 gas, a complex contract much more.

The ticket has two parts.

The base fee is set automatically: if the last bus was more than half full, it rises (by up to 12.5%); if less, it falls. This part is burned — it disappears forever.

The tip is optional and goes to whoever builds the block, to get you on board faster.

EIP-1559 (August 2021). Simplified: real blocks have a target of 50% full.

Watch a rush hour.

Twelve nearly full buses in a row: the base fee climbs about 10% per block — more than three times higher in under 3 minutes. When the rush ends, it slides back down just as fast.

In 2026 a simple transfer costs cents. In busy 2021, it often cost several dollars.

Simplified model: starting fee 1 gwei, ETH at $1,954 (13 Nov 2026).

In short

Each block is a bus with limited seats. The base ticket price rises when buses are full and falls when they're empty — and that base price is burned, not paid to anyone.

Validators each lock 32 ETH as a deposit; every 12 seconds one is picked at random to propose the next page while others vote; a cheater's deposit shrinks in red.

07Act 04 · How it works

Staking: a deposit for honesty

QWithout miners, who keeps the notebook honest?

To help run Ethereum, you put down a deposit.

A 'validator' locks 32 ETH. Every 12 seconds one validator is picked at random to propose the next page; others check it and vote.

About 41.4 million ETH — roughly a third of all ETH — was staked in August 2026.

KuCoin / The Block, Aug 2026.

Carrot and stick.

Honest validators earn new ETH — around 2.8% a year in August 2026. Being offline costs small penalties. Signing two conflicting pages ('slashing') burns part of the deposit and removes the validator.

›Go deeper

Most people don't run their own validator. They stake through exchanges or pools such as Lido, which held roughly 23% of all staked ETH in 2026 — a concentration risk people debate.

One year, three validators.

The honest one ends near 32.9 ETH. The one that is offline a fifth of the time ends near 32.5. The one that double-signs on day 120 is slashed, loses about 1.7 ETH and is removed.

Simplified: ≈2.8% a year; real penalties depend on how many validators are slashed at once.

In short

Validators lock up ETH as a deposit. Honest work earns a small reward; going offline costs a little; cheating can cost a lot. Your own money is the guarantee of your behaviour.

08Act 05 · Money & scale

DeFi: a bank made of code

QCan you borrow money with no bank and no credit check?

Put ETH in, take dollars out.

On lending apps like Aave, you deposit ETH as collateral and borrow stablecoins against it. No forms, no credit score — the code only checks the numbers.

The catch: you must always be over-covered.

You can borrow up to about 80% of your ETH's value. If ETH falls so that your debt reaches about 83% of it, anyone can repay part of your loan and take your ETH at a discount (around 5%). That is a 'liquidation'.

Rounded values similar to Aave v3 for ETH; real parameters vary by market and change over time.

›Go deeper

The 'health factor' = collateral × 83% ÷ debt. Above 1 is safe; at 1 or below, liquidation can happen.

Watch the price fall.

Someone deposits 5 ETH (about $9,770) and borrows $4,690. As ETH slides from $1,954 toward $1,130, the health factor drops from 1.73 to below 1 — and the code lets someone repay half the loan and take ETH at a discount.

In short

DeFi lending works by over-collateral: you lock more than you borrow. If the price of your collateral falls too far, the code sells it automatically — no phone call, no mercy.

09Act 05 · Money & scale

Follow the money

QDoes using Ethereum make ETH scarcer — or more plentiful?

Two taps: one fills, one drains.

Fill: new ETH is created every day to reward stakers. Drain: the base fee of every transaction is burned.

When the network is busy, the drain can be bigger than the tap — supply shrinks. When it's quiet, supply grows.

What really happened since The Merge.

Supply was about 120.5 million ETH at The Merge, fell to about 120.0 million in early 2024, then rose again to about 121.7 million by May 2026 as cheaper Layer-2 networks reduced fees on the main chain.

Coin Metrics, to 23 May 2026. Different trackers report slightly different supply totals.

Four things people mix up.

Network success (people use it) ≠ network revenue (fees paid) ≠ token value (ETH price) ≠ holder profit (what you bought it for). Each can move without the others.

A busy year vs a quiet year.

Like 2023 — about 20 million ETH staked and ~3,000 ETH burned a day: more is burned than created, so supply shrinks a little (≈ −0.3% a year).

Like 2026 — 41 million staked and ~300 burned a day: supply grows about 0.8% a year.

Issuance ≈ 166.3 × √(staked ETH) per year. 2023 fees ≈ 3,600 ETH/day, spring 2026 ≈ 300 (Coin Metrics).

In short

New ETH is created for stakers, and the base fee of every transaction is burned. Busy network → more burned. Quiet network → supply grows. Neither tells you the price.

Hundreds of small transactions are packed into one sealed crate on a side road, and only the crate rides the main Ethereum bus.

10Act 05 · Money & scale

Layer 2: pack the bus

QIf Ethereum is expensive, how did Lan's contract cost only cents?

Instead of one seat each, share a crate.

Layer-2 networks (like Arbitrum or Base) collect hundreds of transactions, pack them into one bundle, and post it to Ethereum. The bus fare is split among everyone in the crate.

2024: a special cheap lane.

The Dencun upgrade added 'blobs', a cheaper space just for these bundles. Fees on Layer 2 dropped to cents — and Fusaka in December 2025 added more room.

Trade-offs.

Many Layer 2s are still run by one company that orders transactions. And because users pay Layer 2 instead of the main chain, less fee money is burned on Ethereum — part of why supply grew again.

In short

Layer-2 networks bundle many transactions and post the bundle to Ethereum. Users get cents-level fees with Ethereum's security behind them — at the price of new trade-offs.

11Act 06 · Reality check

Bitcoin vs Ethereum

QIf Bitcoin is digital gold, what is Ethereum?

Same idea at the start, different goals.

Both are shared records that nobody owns. Bitcoin is built to be simple, scarce money. Ethereum is built to run programs — and ETH is the fuel.

The table below puts them side by side.

Neither one is 'better'.

Bitcoin's strength is that it barely changes; its supply is fixed at 21 million. Ethereum's strength is that it can do much more; its supply has no fixed cap and depends on use. In the year to 13 November 2026, both prices fell — Bitcoin about 31%, ETH about 40%.

Prices: Fortune, 13 Nov 2026. Bitcoin supply: 20 millionth coin mined 9 Mar 2026 (The Block).

In short

Bitcoin does one job — scarce digital money — and changes very slowly. Ethereum is a programmable platform that keeps upgrading. Different goals, different risks.

12Act 06 · Reality check

Reality check

QWhat is strong, what is risky — and what does that mean for ETH?

What's strong.

Running since 2015 without stopping. The largest home for smart contracts and digital dollars — around $157 billion in stablecoins in mid-2026, about half the market. About a third of all ETH staked to secure it.

Axis Intelligence, June 2026; KuCoin, Aug 2026.

What's risky.

Hacks around the network: in February 2025 about $1.5 billion of ETH was stolen from the exchange Bybit. Competitors like Solana are faster and cheaper. Staking is concentrated in a few big providers. And ETH was about 40% lower in November 2026 than a year before.

FBI via TechTarget, Feb 2025; Fortune, 13 Nov 2026.

Both are true at once.

The network kept running through every crash in this story — but that never protected anyone who bought ETH at a peak.

If you are thinking about ETH, ask what you would be relying on: people using the network, fees flowing back to ETH, or simply the price going up. Those are three different bets.

In short

Ethereum is the most-used platform for smart contracts and digital dollars. It also faces hacks around it, strong competitors and concentration — and the price can fall a lot. Both are true.

13Act 06 · Reality check

Back to Lan

QSo — did Lan get paid?

Anna approved. $500 reached Lan in seconds, for a few cents in fees.

No platform cut, no waiting for a bank transfer. The contract did what it promised — on a Layer-2 network secured by Ethereum.

Not magic.

Lan still needs a wallet she protects, and a way to turn digital dollars into đồng. A bug or a lost password can't be called back. Ethereum removes the middleman — it also removes the help desk.

In short

Ethereum turns agreements into code that runs on a computer nobody owns. That is powerful and also unforgiving. Understanding both sides is the point.
