LINK, made simple

One pawnshop, one gold price — and everything Chainlink does, without the jargon.

How can a lending app know today's price?

Khoa runs a pawnshop — on the internet.

Customers leave 1 ETH as a pledge and borrow dollars. The app is a program that runs by itself on a blockchain, with no staff.

🏪
In real life

Like a pawnshop: you leave gold, you borrow cash.

To lend safely, it needs today's price.

If ETH is worth $3,000, the app may lend $2,000. If the price drops, it must know at once.

🏪
In real life

A pawnshop must know today's gold price before handing out cash.

But the blockchain is a sealed glass room.

Programs on a blockchain can only see what is already written inside. They cannot open a website or phone an exchange.

🏪
In real life

A shop with no windows, no phone and no internet.

🔤 Real name: smart contracts can't read off-chain data.

A messenger walks from a gold shop to the glass room carrying a price slip. A second scene: a dishonest messenger brings a fake price and the shop lends too much.

Chapter 02 · The problem

Someone has to bring the price in

Who tells the glass room what gold costs today?

That person is called a price messenger.

Someone outside checks the price and writes it into the blockchain.

🏪
In real life

A runner who goes to the gold shop and comes back with the price.

🔤 Real name: an oracle.

One messenger is risky.

If that one person lies, falls asleep or is bribed, the app lends the wrong amount and someone loses money.

🏪
In real life

One wrong price slip and the pawnshop hands out far too much cash.

Chainlink: many independent messengers.

Chainlink is a network of many separate companies doing this job together, so no single one can cheat or stop the service.

🏪
In real life

Instead of trusting one runner, you send nine who don't work for each other.

Worth knowing

Real companies run Chainlink messengers, including Swisscom and Deutsche Telekom.

Nine messengers each visit a different gold shop. Their prices are lined up; one strange price is thrown out; the middle one is chosen; all nine sign one slip; only that slip enters the glass room.

Chapter 03 · What Chainlink does

Ask nine shops, keep the middle price

If nine messengers bring nine prices, which one is right?

① Each one checks a different shop.

Every messenger collects prices from its own sources, such as different exchanges.

🏪
In real life

Nine runners, nine gold shops.

② Line the prices up, keep the middle.

The prices are sorted and the middle one is chosen. A crazy price at the edge is simply ignored.

🏪
In real life

If eight shops say 100 and one says 92, you don't believe the 92.

🔤 Real name: the median.

③ All of them sign one slip.

Instead of nine separate notes, they agree on one price slip and most of them sign it. The blockchain checks the signatures.

🏪
In real life

One receipt with many stamps is harder to fake than one stamp.

④ Only one slip goes in — so it's cheap.

Writing to a blockchain costs a fee. Sending one signed slip instead of nine cut the messengers' fees by up to 90% (since 2021).

🏪
In real life

One courier trip instead of nine.

🔤 Real name: Off-Chain Reporting (OCR).

Left: a price board on the wall that changes when the price moves enough or every hour. Right: a customer phones for the price at the exact moment of buying.

Chapter 04 · What Chainlink does

A price board, or a phone call?

When does the new price arrive?

The price board on the wall.

The ETH price is updated when it moves 0.5% — or at least every hour. Enough for a pawnshop like Khoa's.

🏪
In real life

The gold price board in a shop window.

🔤 Real name: Data Feeds (push).

The phone call.

Fast trading apps can't wait an hour. They ask for a fresh, signed price at the exact moment of a trade — in under a second.

🏪
In real life

Calling the shop to ask "what's the price right now?" just before you buy.

🔤 Real name: Data Streams (pull).

Same signatures, different delivery.

Both prices are signed by many messengers. The board is always there; the call is faster and paid only when used.

Five glass rooms — Ethereum, Solana, Base, Arbitrum, BNB Chain — each served by the same team of messengers. Chainlink has no room of its own.

Chapter 05 · What Chainlink does

Chainlink owns no shop

Is Chainlink a blockchain like Ethereum or Solana?

Chainlink is not a blockchain.

Ethereum, Solana and BNB Chain are "shops". Chainlink has no shop of its own — its messengers serve dozens of shops at once.

🏪
In real life

Like a delivery company: it owns no stores, it serves all of them.

LINK is the messengers' pay.

LINK is a token that lives on Ethereum. It is used to pay for Chainlink's work and as a deposit messengers put up.

🏪
In real life

The wage you pay the runners.

So it earns differently.

ETH, SOL and BNB earn when people use their blockchain. LINK only earns when someone pays for a Chainlink service.

🏪
In real life

ETH is like rent for the shop; LINK is like the delivery fee.

Worth knowing

That is why the network can be busy while the LINK price stays weak — see chapter 9.

In short

Chainlink is not a blockchain. It is a service that works for many blockchains. LINK is how that service gets paid.

A messenger opens a safe and reports how much is inside; a coin printer stops when the safe isn't full enough. Then a lottery box: the winning number comes with a sealed certificate.

Chapter 06 · What Chainlink does

Checking the safe, drawing lots

What else can the messengers bring in?

Checking the safe.

Some coins promise to be backed by dollars or gold in a safe. Messengers report how much is really in the safe.

🏪
In real life

An inspector who counts the gold in the vault and posts the number.

🔤 Real name: Proof of Reserve.

Not enough in the safe? No new coins.

The coin's program can refuse to print more if the safe doesn't hold enough. Issuers such as 21Shares use this.

🏪
In real life

The printer locks when the vault count is too low.

Fair lottery draws.

Blockchains can't roll dice by themselves. Chainlink brings a random number with a proof; if anyone changes the number, the proof fails.

🏪
In real life

A lottery drawn with a sealed certificate anyone can check.

🔤 Real name: VRF (verifiable randomness).

Two glass rooms joined by a border crossing with two separate checkpoints. Money passes only when both agree; the second checkpoint can close the gate.

Chapter 07 · What Chainlink does

Moving money between shops

How do you move money from one blockchain to another safely?

Old bridges were robbed.

In 2022 thieves stole $624 million from Ronin, $325 million from Wormhole and $190 million from Nomad — all "bridges" between blockchains.

🏪
In real life

A border with one sleepy guard.

Two checkpoints that don't share anything.

In Chainlink's version, one team checks the money, then a second, separate team checks it again with different software. Both must agree.

🏪
In real life

Two customs posts run by different staff, using different machines.

🔤 Real name: CCIP and its Risk Management Network.

The second checkpoint can close the gate.

If it sees something strange, it stops all transfers until the problem is fixed. After other bridges were hacked, projects with over $3 billion moved here.

🏪
In real life

A red barrier that drops across every lane.

In short

Chainlink's transfer service has a second, separate checkpoint that must approve every batch — and can shut the gate if something looks wrong.

Messengers put LINK into a deposit box. The price board stays frozen; a clock reaches three hours; someone rings the bell; each messenger loses 700 LINK and the person who rang gets 7,000 LINK.

Chapter 08 · The LINK token

A deposit — and an alarm bell

What if a messenger stops working?

Messengers leave a deposit.

To take the job, messengers lock up LINK as a deposit. Ordinary holders can add LINK too.

🏪
In real life

A courier leaves a deposit before taking parcels.

🔤 Real name: staking (up to 45 million LINK).

Frozen for 3 hours? Ring the bell.

If the ETH price on Ethereum isn't updated for more than three hours, anyone in the deposit pool can ring the alarm.

🏪
In real life

The price board hasn't changed all afternoon.

Fine for them, reward for you.

Each messenger on that job loses 700 LINK; whoever rang the bell gets 7,000 LINK. Ordinary holders are not fined.

🏪
In real life

Late couriers lose part of their deposit; the person who reported it gets a bonus.

Worth knowing

So far this covers one price (ETH/USD) and the fine is small — a start, not full insurance.

A piggy bank fills slowly with LINK bought from fees. Next to it, a much wider tap pours LINK out of the company's wallets onto an exchange.

Chapter 09 · The LINK token

The LINK token: money in, money out

Does more use mean a higher LINK price?

Only 1 billion LINK will ever exist.

All were created in 2017: 35% sold to the public (about $32 million), 35% kept for messengers and the ecosystem, 30% kept by the company.

🏪
In real life

A fixed box of 1 billion tokens.

Fees are turned into LINK and saved.

Customers can pay in other currencies; the fees are automatically turned into LINK and put in a reserve. By 13 November 2026 it held about 6.68 million LINK.

🏪
In real life

A piggy bank that fills a little every day.

🔤 Real name: Chainlink Reserve.

But a bigger tap is open.

Each quarter about 19 million LINK leave the company's wallets, mostly to the Binance exchange — about 6 million a month, versus about 0.45 million a month going into the piggy bank.

🏪
In real life

Filling a bucket with a cup while a hose drains it.

Worth knowing

About 14 LINK out for every 1 LINK in.

In short

Usage now buys LINK — but about 14 times more LINK flows out to the market than the piggy bank buys.

A price line: 10 cents in 2017, $52.88 in 2021, a stuck price board during the LUNA crash in 2022, and $8.85 in November 2026.

Chapter 10 · The story so far

From 10 cents to today

What happened to the LINK price?

2017: sold at about 10 cents.

The founders sold LINK to the public for roughly 9–11 cents each.

2021: the peak — $52.88.

Lending apps boomed and all needed prices. On 10 May 2021 LINK hit $52.88 — about 580 times the sale price.

2022: a price board got stuck.

When the LUNA coin crashed to $0.01, Chainlink's LUNA board kept showing its built-in minimum of $0.107. Attackers borrowed against the wrong price; the Venus app lost about $11 million.

🏪
In real life

A shop's board still says gold is $100 after it fell to $10.

Worth knowing

Lesson for apps like Khoa's: double-check the price and keep an emergency stop.

2026: about $9.

On 15 November 2026 LINK closed at $8.85 — about 84% below the top — even though more banks and apps use Chainlink than ever.

Worth knowing

Swift is testing it with 17 banks, UBS has done real transactions, and US investors can buy LINK funds (ETFs). Many bank projects are still trials.

Chapter 11 · In short

In short

Is Chainlink good? Is LINK a good buy?

What's strong.

Many independent messengers · one signed price · a second checkpoint for transfers · deposits that can be fined · used by most big lending apps and tested by banks.

What's weak.

LINK only earns from services, and fees can be paid in other coins · about 19 million LINK released every quarter · one company still steers · faster rivals (Pyth, RedStone) · messengers can still be wrong (LUNA).

The service is not the token.

Khoa will use Chainlink prices, plus his own safety checks. If you buy LINK, you bet on the token, not just the service: watch the releases, the piggy bank and how much is paid in LINK. As of November 2026 Vietnam has no licensed crypto exchange. This is education, not investment advice.
