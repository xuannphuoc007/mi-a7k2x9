Intro

USDC, explained

How can a coin always be worth exactly one dollar — and who checks?

No finance knowledge needed. We start with Hà's lanterns.

Hà makes silk lanterns in Hội An and sells them online. Sarah, a customer in Australia, orders 1,000 dollars' worth. A bank transfer would take days and cost fees, so Sarah offers to pay in USDC instead. Scroll down.

USDC is a receipt for a dollar in a vault.

A US company called Circle holds dollars — as cash and as very short loans to the US government. For every dollar it holds, it creates one USDC: a token on a blockchain that can be sent anywhere in minutes.

That is why its price stays at about $1: a business can always hand one USDC back to Circle for one dollar.

Worth knowing

USDC is the second-largest stablecoin. About 67.9 billion were in circulation on 12 November 2026 — a little over a third of the size of Tether's USDT.

So the whole story comes down to two questions: is the money in the vault — and can it always get out?

First we open the vault and see how it works. Then eight years of history — including the weekend in 2023 when part of the vault got stuck in a failing bank — and finally the rules, the strengths, the weaknesses and a conclusion.

In short

USDC is a digital dollar issued by Circle, a US company listed on the stock exchange. Each token is meant to be backed by one real dollar of cash or very short US government debt, and the backing is checked every month.

Two receipts: a business sends Circle 1,000,000 USD and Circle mints 1,000,000 USDC; another returns 1,000,000 USDC, Circle burns them and wires the dollars back. A rubber band keeps the price at $1; a counter shows about 67.9 billion USDC.

2How the vault works

Dollars in, tokens out

Where do new USDC come from — and where do they go?

Send a dollar, a token is minted.

A business with a Circle Mint account — say a payments company — wires Circle 1 million dollars. Circle creates ("mints") 1 million new USDC and sends them to the company's wallet.

Nothing magical: the dollars go into the reserve, the tokens go out into the world.

Hand the token back, it is burned.

When a business returns 1 million USDC, Circle wires the dollars back and destroys ("burns") the tokens. Only verified businesses can do this directly with Circle; a standard redemption carries no Circle fee.

So the number of USDC should always match the dollars Circle owes.

Worth knowing

The door is busy: in the week to 12 November 2026, Circle minted about 10.8 billion USDC and burned about 12.1 billion. At that pace, an amount equal to every USDC in existence passes through Circle in about six weeks.

Sarah never meets Circle. So why is her USDC worth $1?

Ordinary people buy USDC on an exchange or in an app. Big traders keep that price near $1: if USDC drops to $0.99, they buy the cheap tokens and hand them to Circle for a full dollar; if it rises to $1.01, they mint new ones for $1 and sell them.

That simple trade works like a rubber band, pulling the price back to one dollar.

About 67.9 billion USDC exist today.

On 12 November 2026 Circle reported about 67.9 billion USDC in circulation, against reserves of about $68.1 billion.

Each one is a promise of one dollar. Next: what exactly backs those promises?

In short

New USDC are created when dollars come in and destroyed when dollars go out. Traders keep the price near $1 by buying cheap tokens and cashing them in, or creating new ones and selling them.

3How the vault works

Open the vault: cash and very short IOUs, nothing else

What backs 67.9 billion promises?

$68.1 billion of reserves.

Circle publishes what is in the vault every week, and every month a Big Four accounting firm (Deloitte) checks that the reserves are at least as large as the USDC in circulation. On 12 November 2026: $68.1 billion of reserves for about 67.9 billion USDC.

Let's turn that into 100 squares, each worth about $0.68 billion.

59 squares: overnight loans backed by US government bonds.

The biggest part — about $39.9 billion — is "repo": Circle lends cash overnight to large banks and takes US Treasury bonds as a pledge. The next morning the cash comes back.

Most of this sits in the Circle Reserve Fund, a money-market fund registered with the US securities regulator and managed by BlackRock, the world's largest asset manager. Circle is its only shareholder.

Worth knowing

BlackRock publishes the fund's holdings itself — a second pair of eyes, outside Circle, on most of the vault.

29 squares: Treasury bills.

About $20.1 billion is US Treasury bills that mature in less than three months — short IOUs from the US government.

Worth knowing

S&P found that the vault's assets mature, on average, in about ten days. In other words, almost the whole vault turns back into cash within two weeks.

12 squares: cash in banks.

About $7.4 billion sits as deposits at "systemically important" banks — the giants governments are least likely to let fail — and about $0.6 billion at other banks.

Cash is available at once on a weekday. But a deposit is only as safe as the bank that holds it.

About 100 squares are owed. Now freeze the banks.

Reserves and USDC are almost equal: $68.1 billion against 67.9 billion. Unlike Tether, Circle keeps almost no extra cushion inside the vault. Its promise is simply one dollar in, one dollar out — checked every month.

Stress test: imagine every bank holding Circle's cash froze it for a weekend.

Circle transparency report, 12 Nov 2026. A monthly accountant's check confirms the totals on given dates; it is not a full audit of the company.

Worth knowing

Even then, 88 of the 100 squares — the repo loans and Treasury bills — would still be there. In 2023 the stuck share was about 8%, and that alone pushed USDC down to 87 cents. Chapter 5 tells that story.

In short

Almost nine tenths of the vault is very short lending to, or secured by, the US government; the rest is bank cash. No gold, no bitcoin, no company loans — but also almost no extra cushion.

Hà's 1,000 USDC earn 0%; the dollars behind them earn interest for Circle. Bars show Circle's revenue: $1.45 billion (2023), $1.68 billion (2024), $2.75 billion (2025), $2.26 billion (January–September 2026). Of every 100 dollars earned in 2025, about 61 were paid on to partners, mostly Coinbase.

4How the vault works

Interest for Circle — and a big share for Coinbase

If Hà's USDC earns nothing, who earns the interest?

Hà earns 0%. Circle earns the interest.

Holding USDC pays no interest from Circle. But the dollars behind it sit in Treasury bills and repo loans that do pay interest — about 3.5% a year in the third quarter of 2026.

So the more USDC exists, the more Circle earns.

Billions a year — nearly all of it interest.

Circle's revenue was about $1.45 billion in 2023, $1.68 billion in 2024 and $2.75 billion in 2025. In 2024, 99% of it came from interest on the reserves.

From January to September 2026 it reached about $2.26 billion, including a one-off $182 million from pre-selling tokens of its new blockchain, Arc.

But most of the interest is passed on.

Circle pays exchanges and apps to hold and offer USDC. In 2025, about 61 of every 100 dollars it earned went straight out again as distribution and transaction costs.

The biggest partner by far is Coinbase, which helped launch USDC and received about $908 million from Circle in 2024 alone.

Worth knowing

In 2024 Circle paid Coinbase about $908 million — almost six times Circle's own net profit that year (about $156 million).

The business in one line.

Take dollars from holders at 0%, lend them to the US government, share the interest with the distributors.

When US rates fall, income falls with them: Circle estimated that one percentage point lower would cut its reserve income by about $441 million a year. In the third quarter of 2026, a 0.61-point drop in its return already cut reserve income by 8%.

In short

USDC holders get no interest from Circle. Circle earns it from the reserves — then pays most of it to the exchanges and apps that spread USDC, above all Coinbase.

A timeline: Circle founded in 2013, USDC launched on 26 September 2018, Circle listed on the NYSE on 5 June 2025. Sarah's bank transfer crawls to Hà while USDC zips across; Circle and Coinbase under the Centre consortium, 2018–2023; the early supply line stays below $1 billion until mid-2020, then jumps to about $4 billion by 2021.

5Eight years

From a payments start-up to the New York Stock Exchange

Who built USDC, and why?

2013: a payments start-up. 2018: a digital dollar.

Circle was founded in 2013 by Jeremy Allaire and Sean Neville. On 26 September 2018, together with the exchange Coinbase, it launched USDC on Ethereum — a dollar token backed by cash and reviewed by accountants every month.

Worth knowing

USDC arrived four years after Tether, from the opposite starting point: designed from day one to live inside US rules.

Why a token at all? Speed.

A bank transfer from Sarah in Australia to Hà in Vietnam can take days and pass through several banks, each taking a fee. USDC goes from wallet to wallet in minutes, at any hour, on any day.

Circle + Coinbase = Centre.

At first USDC was run by Centre, a consortium set up by Circle and Coinbase. In August 2023 they closed it: Circle took full control of USDC, and Coinbase received a stake in Circle while keeping a share of the interest.

Worth knowing

That 2023 deal is why Coinbase still collects such a large share of USDC's interest (chapter 3).

A slow start, a jump — and Wall Street.

For almost two years USDC stayed below $1 billion. Then the March 2020 crash and "DeFi summer" — apps on Ethereum that lend and trade without a bank — pushed it to about $4 billion by 1 January 2021.

On 5 June 2025 Circle listed its shares on the New York Stock Exchange: priced at $31, they closed the first day at $83.23.

Worth knowing

That 168% first-day jump was the biggest for a billion-dollar US IPO in more than 30 years of Renaissance Capital's records.

In short

USDC was built by Circle, with Coinbase, as a regulated digital dollar that accountants can check. It grew slowly at first, took off with DeFi in 2020, and Circle went public in 2025.

Silicon Valley Bank sinks below the waterline with $3.3 billion of USDC reserves inside; USDC drops to about $0.87 on 11 March 2023 and returns to $1; the supply bar shrinks from about $43 billion to about $32.5 billion; two tanks: SVB collapsed, USDC kept paying $1.

6Eight years

When the bank holding the cash failed

What happens if the bank holding the dollars goes bust?

Friday, 10 March 2023: Silicon Valley Bank collapses.

SVB, a large Californian bank, failed after a run by its depositors. Late that night Circle said $3.3 billion of its roughly $40 billion of reserves was still inside — the transfers it had ordered to move the money had not gone through.

Saturday: USDC falls to 87 cents.

Banks were closed for the weekend, so Circle could not pay anyone out. Holders who wanted to leave had to sell on exchanges, and the price fell to about $0.87 — the lowest in USDC's history.

Worth knowing

Markets did the arithmetic fast: about 8% of the reserves were stuck, and the price fell by roughly that much — and briefly more.

Monday: the doors reopen.

On Sunday the US authorities said all SVB deposits would be protected. When banks opened on Monday, Circle restarted redemptions: from 13 to 15 March it paid out $3.8 billion and created $0.8 billion of new USDC. The price went back to $1.

But trust had taken a hit: within weeks about $10 billion of USDC had been cashed in and did not come back.

A bank died; USDC did not.

SVB lost about a quarter of its deposits in a single day and collapsed. USDC lost about a quarter of its supply over the following weeks — and still paid $1 for every token handed back.

Worth knowing

Circle's lesson: since then it keeps most of the vault in a BlackRock fund of US government debt and its cash at the very largest banks. But the 2023 rescue came from the US government, not from Circle.

In short

In March 2023 about 8% of USDC's reserves got stuck in a failed bank. The price fell to 87 cents for a weekend and recovered once the US government guaranteed the bank's deposits. Circle then moved its cash to the largest banks.

7Eight years

Shrinking by half — then a comeback

How big is USDC now, and where does it live?

Up to $56 billion — then down to $24 billion.

USDC peaked at about $56 billion in June 2022. The crypto crash, the SVB weekend and competition from USDT then shrank it to about $23.8 billion at the start of 2024.

The line joins dated figures from Circle and the press with straight lines; it is not a daily series.

The comeback.

With clearer rules in Europe and the US, USDC grew again: about $44 billion at the start of 2025 and $75.3 billion at its end. In late 2026 it slipped back, to about 67.9 billion in mid-November.

Worth knowing

In 2025 alone USDC grew 72% — faster than USDT. Even so, USDT is still more than two and a half times its size.

One coin, 38 blockchains.

Circle issues USDC directly on 38 blockchains (September 2026). About 60% of it sits on Ethereum; Hyperliquid and Base are next.

To move USDC between chains, Circle burns it on one and mints it on the other — a system called CCTP. More than $41 billion moved that way in the last quarter of 2025 alone.

Like USDT, it can be frozen.

Circle can block any address, and does so on court orders, sanctions and regulators' demands. From 2023 to 2025 it blacklisted about 372 addresses holding $109 million. Tether blacklisted about 7,268 holding $3.29 billion.

Worth knowing

Circle freezes about 30 times less than Tether — but a freeze can still hit businesses: in March 2026 it froze 16 business wallets at once in a civil court case.

A public company in New York.

Circle is listed on the New York Stock Exchange (ticker CRCL) and files audited yearly accounts and quarterly reports with the US securities regulator. Anyone can read how much it earns, what it pays Coinbase and what sits in the vault.

In short

USDC fell by more than half from 2022 to 2024, then tripled. It lives on 38 blockchains, mostly Ethereum, and — like USDT — it can be frozen, though far less often.

America's GENIUS Act demands licences, full safe reserves and regular reports; Circle wins a federal trust-bank charter. Europe's MiCA shelf keeps USDC while USDT slides off. S&P's gauge points to 2, "strong"; USDT sits at 5.

8USDC today

The rule-follower's advantage

Is USDC allowed in the US and Europe?

America: the GENIUS Act — and a bank charter.

On 18 July 2025 the US signed its first federal stablecoin law: issuers serving Americans need a licence, full reserves of safe assets and regular reports — close to how USDC already worked.

Circle also applied to open a national trust bank. The US bank regulator, the OCC, approved it conditionally in December 2025 and finally on 10 July 2026: Circle National Trust, under direct federal supervision.

Worth knowing

The trust bank starts by safekeeping digital assets; the plan is for it to oversee the USDC reserve later — moving the vault under a federal bank supervisor.

Europe: first through the door.

The EU's MiCA rules allow only licensed stablecoins on regulated exchanges. On 1 July 2024 Circle became the first global stablecoin issuer to comply, through an e-money licence in France. Tether did not apply, and USDT was removed for European users.

A "strong" grade.

The rating agency S&P scores how well a stablecoin holds $1, from 1 (very strong) to 5 (weak). In December 2025 it kept USDC at 2, "strong": the reserves themselves scored 1, the best, but it is not yet proven how holders would be protected if Circle itself went bankrupt. USDT is rated 5.

9Strengths & weaknesses

What USDC does well

Why do banks, apps and payment companies choose it?

1 · The plainest vault.

Only cash, overnight loans backed by Treasury bonds, and Treasury bills — no gold, bitcoin or company loans. S&P gives the reserve assets its best score: 1, "very strong".

2 · Checked every month.

Circle shows the vault every week, a Big Four firm checks it every month, and as a listed company Circle files audited accounts with the US securities regulator.

3 · Inside the rules.

Licensed under Europe's MiCA since July 2024, built for America's GENIUS Act, and with a federally supervised trust bank approved in July 2026.

4 · Fast and everywhere.

Issued natively on 38 blockchains and moved between them by burning and minting — more than $41 billion in the last quarter of 2025 alone.

5 · It survived its worst weekend.

In March 2023, with $3.3 billion stuck in a failed bank, Circle still paid out $3.8 billion in three days once banks reopened — and every token went back to $1.

Five warning cards: it depends on banks; one company can freeze it; holders get no interest; income tied to US rates; smaller and swings with crypto.

10Strengths & weaknesses

What can go wrong

What risks come with holding USDC?

1 · It depends on banks.

The 2023 weekend showed it: when one bank failed, USDC fell to 87 cents. Bank cash — about 12% of the vault today — is only as safe as the banks that hold it, and redemptions wait for banks to open.

2 · One company can freeze it.

Circle blocks addresses on court orders and sanctions. It does so far less often than Tether, but any USDC can still be frozen.

3 · No interest, no insurance.

Holders earn nothing from Circle while it keeps the interest and shares most of it with Coinbase and other partners. And USDC is not a bank deposit: there is no deposit insurance.

4 · Tied to US interest rates.

Nearly all Circle's income is interest. Each one-point cut in US rates costs it about $441 million a year — pressure that grows as rates fall.

5 · Smaller, and swings with crypto.

USDC fell from about $56 billion (2022) to $23.8 billion (2024) before recovering. USDT is still more than two and a half times larger and dominates trading.

11Conclusion

Conclusion

So, should Hà accept USDC?

USDC does one job well: a dollar you can check.

It has stayed at about $1 for eight years except for one weekend, keeps a simple vault of cash and US government debt, is checked every month and is regulated in the US and Europe.

But it is still a promise from one company — and from its banks.

Circle sets the rules, can freeze addresses, keeps the interest and depends on the banking system. Regulation lowers these risks; it does not remove them.

Worth knowing

Choosing between USDC and USDT is a trade-off: USDC offers more rules and openness; USDT offers more size and reach. Neither pays holders interest or insures their money.

For Hà: a tool, not a savings account.

To get paid by Sarah in minutes, USDC is practical. To spend the money, Hà still has to turn it into đồng — and as of November 2026 no crypto exchange in Vietnam is licensed yet. So convert promptly, use reputable platforms and don't leave large sums on one exchange. This is education, not investment advice.
