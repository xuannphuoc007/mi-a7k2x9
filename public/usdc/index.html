01 · The birth of digital money

What if a dollar could travel like data?

The Journey of a Digital Dollar. Follow the money. Discover what happens behind every USDC.

Scroll to follow the dollar

Meet Hà. She sells handmade lanterns online from Hội An. A customer in Australia, Sarah, has just paid her 1,000 USDC — and Hà wants to know what she actually received.

Like a parking ticket

USDC works like a parking ticket. You leave a dollar, you get a ticket. The ticket isn’t the dollar — but whoever holds it can get the dollar back.

Reported · ≈ 67.9 billion USDC in circulation · 12 Nov 2026

Paper becomes code.

USDC rewrites that dollar as a token — a balance on a blockchain that can move at any hour, to anyone with an address.

›How it works

USDC is a stablecoin issued by Circle, a US company listed in New York. Each token aims to be worth exactly one US dollar, and Circle's eligible business customers can swap it back for one dollar.

Not a bank deposit. Not a Fed dollar.

Money in a bank account is a promise from the bank, usually insured up to $250,000. A digital dollar from the Federal Reserve would be a promise from the state. USDC is a promise from Circle, backed by a pool of reserves — with no deposit insurance.

+Deep dive
  • Who issues it: Circle Internet Group (NYSE: CRCL). Circle mints new USDC when a customer sends dollars, and burns USDC when it pays dollars back.
  • Where it lives: Circle issues USDC natively on 30 blockchains (as of 6 Feb 2026), including Ethereum, Solana and Base.
  • What it is not: not FDIC-insured, not legal tender, not a central bank digital currency.
Worth knowing

USDC is busier than it is big. In Q3 2026 Circle created about $104 billion of new USDC and destroyed about $104 billion — while the total in circulation barely moved. Dollars pour in and out of this system every day.

Follow the dollar.

Down to the floor where it is minted, into the vault that backs it, across the blockchains — and back.

The coin is a metaphor. USDC is fungible: no token is a particular banknote, and no single dollar physically travels through the system. We follow one unit of value to make the machinery visible.

02 · The minting machine

How does a dollar become USDC?

Reported · Circle Mint: institutions only, not individuals · Oct 2026

A business sends real dollars to Circle by bank wire.

Like a parking ticket

A car park prints a ticket only when a car drives in. Circle prints USDC only when real dollars arrive.

›How it works

Only approved institutions — exchanges, banks, wallet providers, trading firms — can mint directly. They pass a business identity check (KYB) and wire US dollars from their own bank.

The press runs only when dollars arrive.

When the wire lands, Circle creates exactly the same number of new USDC: $1,000 in, 1,000 USDC out.

›How it works

The new tokens are written onto the blockchain the customer picks. Circle Mint charges no spread and no per-transaction fee to issue.

1 : 1, whatever the amount.

$100, $1,000 or $10,000 — the rule never changes.

Worth knowing

Circle says more than 333 billion USDC have been minted through Circle Mint since it opened in 2018 — nearly five times all the USDC that exists today. Most of it has already been redeemed and destroyed again.

Buying on an exchange is different.

That’s how Sarah got hers: she bought USDC on an exchange. Nothing new was printed — someone else’s USDC simply changed hands.

Like a parking ticket

Buying a ticket from another driver doesn’t put a new car in the car park.

›How it works

This is how most people get USDC. Only the exchange — or another institution — deals with Circle directly.

One floor down: the vault.

Every dollar that came in has to be kept somewhere.

03 · The reserve vault

Where is the money behind USDC?

Reported · Reserves ≈ $68.1B vs ≈ 67.9B USDC · 12 Nov 2026

Under every USDC there is a vault. It holds almost no banknotes. It holds short loans — to the US government and to the biggest banks.

Like a parking ticket

Every car that comes in is parked somewhere. Hà’s 1,000 USDC is backed by the whole car park — not by one numbered space.

≈ $39.9B · overnight loans

The biggest block, about 59%. Every night this money is lent to large banks, who hand over US Treasury bonds as a guarantee. Next morning the cash comes back — and is lent again.

›How it works

Finance calls this an overnight reverse repurchase agreement (reverse repo). It matures in one day and is secured by Treasuries.

+Deep dive
  • Why hold it: it pays interest, and it turns back into cash every single day.
  • Risk: it relies on the banks on the other side and on the collateral being handled correctly. If a bank failed, Circle would keep the Treasuries — which then have to be sold.

≈ $20.1B · Treasury bills

About 30% sits in IOUs from the US government that are paid back within three months.

›How it works

Because they mature so soon, their price barely moves when interest rates change, so they can be sold quickly at close to face value.

+Deep dive
  • Risk: small, not zero. Selling tens of billions in a panic can still cost a little, and the cash only arrives on the next settlement.

≈ $8.0B · cash in banks

About 12% is plain cash, ready for days when many holders want their dollars back at once.

›How it works

≈ $7.4B sits in 'systemically important' banks — the giants regulators consider too important to fail. ≈ $0.6B is in other banks.

+Deep dive
  • Risk: a bank deposit above the insurance limit can be frozen if the bank fails. That exact thing happened to USDC in March 2023.

One pool, not millions of lockers.

Most of the loans and Treasury bills sit inside one fund — the Circle Reserve Fund, run by BlackRock. No USDC owns a particular bill. Every token is a claim on the whole pool.

›How it works

The fund is registered with the US SEC as a government money market fund (ticker USDXX). Circle is its only shareholder, and its holdings are published every business day.

Worth knowing

More than half of the money behind USDC is lent out overnight — every night. The vault is less a safe than a machine that lends and collects back every 24 hours. That machine is also Circle's business: reserve income was $657.6M in Q3 2026, about 76% of its revenue.

04 · The internet of dollars

One dollar. Many blockchains.

Reported · Native on 30 blockchains · 6 Feb 2026

USDC doesn’t live on one blockchain. Circle issues it directly on dozens — each one like a different town, with its own roads and tolls. Hà’s wallet can hold it on Ethereum, Base or Solana.

Like a parking ticket

The same ticket is accepted at car parks in many towns.

Base

Coinbase’s network: cheap and busy. Here, as on Ethereum, USDC is a standard ERC-20 token.

Worth knowing

Most USDC sits on Ethereum — about two-thirds of the supply in April 2026. But the busiest roads are elsewhere: Solana carries the most transfers, Base the most dollars moved.

Arbitrum

Until June 2023, USDC here was a copy carried over a bridge. Now Circle issues it on Arbitrum directly.

Native vs bridged

A bridge locks real USDC in its own vault and hands out a look-alike: USDC.e.

Like a parking ticket

USDC.e is like a ticket re-issued by a middleman car park. It is only as good as the middleman.

›How it works

USDC.e is a claim on the bridge, not on Circle. If the bridge fails or is hacked, the copy can lose its backing — and Circle does not redeem it.

+Deep dive
  • The “.e” means “bridged from Ethereum”. It appeared on Avalanche, Arbitrum, Optimism and Polygon before Circle arrived there natively.
  • Circle’s Bridged USDC Standard lets a new chain start with a bridged token that Circle can later upgrade in place to native USDC. World Chain did this in June 2025.

Polygon · Avalanche

Different speeds, different fees. Fees change minute by minute, so we don’t show a fixed number.

›How it works

Each chain charges fees in its own token, set by traffic. How quickly a payment becomes final also differs: seconds on some chains, minutes on Ethereum and the networks built on it.

So how does USDC travel between towns?

Not over a bridge. It is burned on one side and issued on the other.

05 · The cross-chain portal

How can USDC move between blockchains?

Hà wants her USDC on Solana, where sending it costs less than a cent. But blockchains cannot see each other — so ‘moving’ USDC is really two separate events, and someone has to vouch for the first.

Like a parking ticket

To move a ticket to another car park: the first one tears it up and phones ahead, the second one prints a fresh ticket.

Illustration · Example route: Ethereum → Solana · no real transfer

Step 1 · Burn

1,000 USDC on Ethereum is destroyed. Not sent — destroyed.

›How it works

The sender calls Circle's contract (depositForBurn). The tokens leave existence on Ethereum, and the transaction records where they should reappear.

Step 2 · Attest

Circle signs a message that says: this burn really happened.

›How it works

Circle's attestation service (called Iris) watches the source chain and signs once the burn is confirmed. Fast Transfer signs as soon as the block lands — about 20 seconds from Ethereum — for a fee of 0.01%. Standard waits for full finality, about 15–19 minutes from Ethereum.

What travels is a message, not money.

For these seconds or minutes the 1,000 USDC exists nowhere. There is only a signed promise in flight.

Worth knowing

CCTP has no bridge vault in the middle — the place hackers usually attack. Instead, all trust sits in one spot: Circle's signature. Without it, nothing is minted. In 2025, more than $41 billion moved this way, 3.7× the year before.

Step 3 · Mint

Solana checks Circle's signature and creates 1,000 brand-new USDC.

›How it works

Anyone can deliver the signed message to the destination contract (receiveMessage). Total supply is back where it was: −1,000 on Ethereum, +1,000 on Solana.

+Deep dive
  • CCTP V2 launched in March 2025 on Ethereum, Avalanche and Base; Solana joined V2 in October 2025.
  • Fees: Standard Transfer has no Circle fee on most routes (you still pay network gas). Fast Transfer adds a Circle fee set per source chain — 1 basis point from Ethereum or Solana.
  • The destination USDC is native: the same token Circle issues there directly, not a wrapped copy.

Burn-and-mint vs lock-and-mint

Why it matters which road your USDC took.

CCTP · burn and mint
1Burn on chain A
2Circle signs
3Native USDC minted on chain B

You hold real USDC on both sides. Trust: Circle's attestation key.

Typical bridge · lock and mint
1USDC locked in a bridge vault
2Bridge mints an IOU
3Wrapped token (e.g. USDC.e) on chain B

You hold an IOU on the bridge's vault. If the vault is hacked, the IOU can lose its value.

06 · The one-dollar question

Why doesn’t USDC always trade at exactly $1?

Illustration · Price paths in this chapter are simulations

Hà sells some USDC on an exchange to pay her suppliers. Like anything traded, its price can wobble a little.

Like a parking ticket

Tickets can be resold. If one goes for 99 cents, someone buys it and collects the full dollar at the car park — so the price comes back.

›How it works

Two worlds meet here: the market, where anyone trades, and Circle’s counter, where approved institutions swap 1 : 1 for dollars.

Normal day

If USDC slips to $0.999, a trader buys it cheap and redeems it at Circle for $1. Above $1, they mint and sell. Those trades pull the price back.

›How it works

It only pays when the gap is bigger than the costs: Circle’s fee (Standard redemption has none, Express is 0.05%), bank transfer time and network fees.

Liquidity shortage

When many sell at once and few buyers are around, the price dips further — until enough arbitrage arrives.

Counter closed

If redemptions stop — a weekend, a bank problem — the pull disappears. Nothing forces the price back to $1.

›How it works

In March 2023 redemptions were restricted over the weekend because Circle’s partner banks were closed.

Worth knowing

Arbitrage is a rubber band, not a guarantee. It needs an open counter, working banks and traders willing to wait for their money.

Who can stop a USDC?

Look underneath the token.

07 · X-ray: the hidden control layer

Digital money has rules.

Behind every USDC is a smart contract with special keys — and Circle holds all of them. That’s why Hà only takes payments from customers she knows.

Like a parking ticket

The car park can block a ticket reported stolen: whoever holds it can no longer get a car out.

›How it works

The contract has an owner, a master minter who decides who may create tokens, a pauser, a blacklister and an admin who can upgrade the code.

Reported · ≈ 372 addresses · ≈ $109M frozen · 2023–2025

Freeze

Circle can put an address on a blacklist. The balance stays visible but can no longer be sent or received.

›How it works

Circle’s USDC Terms (updated 15 Oct 2026) reserve the right to block addresses Circle believes are linked to illegal activity.

The transfer bounces.

Any new transfer to or from that address fails. Past transfers are not undone — the blockchain record stays as it was.

The bigger switches

The pauser can stop every transfer at once; the admin can replace the contract’s code. Rarely used — but they exist.

Worth knowing

USDC freezes far less than USDT: about 372 addresses and $109M in 2023–2025, against 7,268 addresses and $3.29B for Tether. Yet in March 2026 Circle froze 16 business wallets at once in a sealed civil case — and unfroze one after public backlash.

Rules matter most when trust breaks.

March 2023 was that test.

08 · When the peg breaks

What happens when trust is tested?

Reported · Historical · 10–16 March 2023

Friday, 10 March 2023

US regulators close Silicon Valley Bank. Circle’s wires to move cash out have not gone through.

Like a parking ticket

Part of the cars sat in a side car park that was sealed on a Friday night — and the main office was closed all weekend. People sold their tickets cheap.

›How it works

$3.3B of USDC’s ≈ $40B reserve is stuck there — about 8%.

Saturday: $0.87

Holders rush to sell on exchanges. With banks shut for the weekend, nobody can redeem at Circle, and USDC falls to about $0.87. Had Hà needed cash that Saturday, her 1,000 USDC would have fetched about $870.

Sunday: a promise

The US Treasury, Fed and FDIC say every SVB depositor will be made whole. Circle says the $3.3B will be available when banks open.

Monday: the counter reopens

Redemptions restart. From 13 to 15 March Circle redeems $3.8B and mints $0.8B. USDC is back above $0.99.

The peg came back. Some trust didn’t.

Over the next two weeks USDC’s market value fell by about $10B.

Worth knowing

USDC never lacked assets — 92% of the reserve was untouched. It broke because 8% sat in one bank over a weekend. The vault changed afterwards: most of the reserve now sits in a government money-market fund rather than in bank deposits.

09 · Back to the real world

Can the digital dollar become a dollar again?

Reported · Standard: no Circle fee · Express: 0.05% · from 19 Oct 2026

Hà sells her USDC to an exchange; the exchange — an approved institution — hands USDC back to Circle.

Like a parking ticket

Hand back the ticket, get your car. Only regular customers deal with the office directly — everyone else goes through an exchange.

›How it works

Direct redemption is for Circle Mint customers only — exchanges, banks, fintechs, trading firms. Individuals usually sell their USDC on an exchange or in an app instead.

Burn

The returned USDC is destroyed, and the matching dollars leave the reserve.

Wire

The dollars travel to the customer’s bank by wire — on banking days, at bank speed.

›How it works

Settlement time depends on the banks and payment rails involved; Express is faster, for a fee.

+Deep dive
  • Who you depend on: Circle and its terms; its partner banks and the reserve fund; the blockchain your USDC sits on; and, if you are not a Circle Mint customer, the exchange or app you sell through.
  • In Vietnam, people usually turn USDC into đồng through exchanges or peer-to-peer trades. As of mid-November 2026, no domestic crypto exchange had yet been licensed under the 2025 pilot.

All the way back.

From the counter, over the chains and the vault, up to the table where it started.

One dollar again.

Hà’s 1,000 USDC is money again — enough for next month’s silk and bamboo. USDC works because, at the end of the road, someone hands you a dollar.

Worth knowing

The real question about USDC is not “is it backed?” — on 12 Nov 2026 it was, slightly over 100%. It is “how fast, for whom, and on which days can it become a dollar?”
